[Safety] How Reasoning Models Are Kept Safe: A Map by Approach

A map of how safety is achieved in autoregressive reasoning models: training the reasoning, guiding it at inference, monitoring it, and containing what is exposed, plus the tensions between them.

TL;DR

Long chain-of-thought models such as o1 and DeepSeek-R1 created a safety problem that chat models did not have. The model now writes a long trace before it answers, and that trace can be unsafe even when the answer is a refusal. Hidden Risks of LRMs found the thinking process more dangerous than the final answer, and Chain of Risk gave the two failure types names: a leak is an unsafe trace with a safe answer, and an escape is a benign trace with an unsafe answer.

The defenses fall into four families, sorted by where the control acts:

Family Where control acts Representative work
A. Train the reasoning The weights Deliberative Alignment, STAR-1, SafePath, IPO, SaLT-DPO
B. Guide the reasoning Decoding, weights frozen Thinking Intervention, ReasoningGuard, activation steering, Saffron-1
C. Monitor the reasoning An external observer ReasoningShield, CoT monitors, activation probes
D. Contain the reasoning What is exposed Hiding or summarizing the trace

A second axis cuts across all four: which object is secured, the trace, the answer, or both. Most early methods secured only the answer, and most recent ones target the trace.

A. Train the reasoning

The idea is to change the weights so the model’s own chain of thought does the safety work: recall a policy, notice the risk, then refuse or recover. Methods differ mainly in where along the trace the training signal lands.

A1. Recall the policy

These methods teach the model a written safety policy and train it to reason over that policy before answering.

  • Deliberative Alignment (Guan et al., 2024) is the reference point. The model is taught the safety spec as text and trained to recall it in its CoT. No human-written reasoning is needed. OpenAI reports better jailbreak robustness together with less overrefusal.
  • STAR-1 (Wang et al., 2025) shows how far a small amount of data goes: 1K policy-grounded reasoning samples give a 40% average safety gain across four benchmarks, for a 1.1% average drop in reasoning ability.
  • RealSafe-R1 (Zhang et al., 2025) fine-tunes R1 on 15K trajectories that R1 generated itself, so the training data stays in the model’s own distribution and reasoning is preserved.
  • SafeChain (Jiang et al., 2025) released a CoT-style safety SFT set. SRG (Wang et al., ICML 2025) made a related observation: Best-of-N safety keeps rising with N, so the safety knowledge is present but under-elicited.

A2. Anchor the start of the trace

These methods put the safety signal at the very beginning of the reasoning and leave the rest alone.

  • SafePath (Jeung et al., NeurIPS 2025) trains the model to emit an 8-token “safety primer” at the start of its reasoning on harmful prompts. On R1-Distill-Llama-8B it cuts harmful responses by up to 90% and blocks 83.3% of jailbreaks, with 295.9× less compute than training direct refusals.
  • SafeKey (Zhou et al., 2025) targets the “key sentence” that follows the model’s restatement of the query, the point where it either recognizes the risk or does not. It lowers the average harmfulness rate by 9.6%.

Anchoring the start is cheap. The weakness is that the remainder of the trace is unsupervised, which the next group addresses.

A3. Supervise each step and train recovery

The common observation behind these methods is that models often notice harm and then reason their way past it. Chain-of-Guardrail (Mao et al., ACL 2026) calls this self-jailbreak.

  • R2D (Zhu et al., EMNLP 2025) adds “safety pivot tokens” for step-wise self-evaluation.
  • IPO (Zhang et al., ICLR 2026) builds preference pairs by replacing compliant steps with safety triggers and reports a relative harmfulness reduction of over 30%.
  • RECAP (Peng et al., 2025) prefills deliberately flawed reasoning during RL so the model learns to reject it. Self-ReSET (Zhang et al., 2026) starts RL from the model’s own unsafe trajectories and trains recovery.
  • STAIR (Zhang et al., ICML 2025 Oral) builds step-level preference data with safety-informed MCTS. Its process reward model also drives test-time search.
  • The precursor is Backtracking (Zhang et al., 2024), on chat models: a learned [RESET] token lets the model discard an unsafe partial generation, taking unsafe outputs from 6.1% to 1.5%.

A4. Secure the trace directly

Answer-only rewards leave unsafe traces untouched. SaLT-DPO (Yun et al., EMNLP 2026) scores the reasoning and the answer as separate segments and adds a cross-segment consistency term, which lowers unsafe rates in both.

A contrasting design is Answer-Then-Check (Cao et al., ICLR 2026). The model drafts an answer inside its thoughts, checks it, and only then decides what to output. It trades a clean trace for a better final decision.

What training buys and costs. Safety travels with the weights, including open weights, and inference stays cheap. The price is the safety tax: sequential safety alignment degrades reasoning. Overrefusal and dependence on the training distribution are further costs.

B. Guide the reasoning at inference

Here the weights stay frozen. Decoding is steered through the thought tokens, the thinking budget, the activations, or search.

  • Edit the thought. Thinking Intervention (Wu et al., 2025) inserts or revises thinking tokens before the answer, raising refusal on unsafe prompts by 40% for open R1 models. ReasoningGuard (Wang et al., 2025) uses attention to find critical points in the trace, injects a safety reflection there, and reports outperforming nine existing safeguards.
  • Control the budget. SafeChain’s ZeroThink, LessThink and MoreThink vary how much the model thinks. Turning thinking off is a crude but real lever.
  • Steer activations. Steering builds on the finding that refusal is mediated by a single direction (Arditi et al., 2024) across 13 open chat models. For reasoning models, Chain of Risk learns one unsafe-to-safe direction per safety principle and reports a 40.8% reduction in unsafe outputs while keeping 97.7% of accuracy. SafeSwitch (Han et al., 2025) turns on a safety head only when a probe detects harmful intent, cutting harmful outputs by about 80%. It sits between this family and the next.
  • Search. Saffron-1 (Qiu et al., 2025) finds that off-the-shelf inference scaling helps safety less than Best-of-N does, and proposes tree search guided by a dedicated “multifurcation” reward model.

What inference-time guidance buys and costs. It needs no retraining and can be switched on per request. It costs latency and compute, and steering requires white-box access. Interventions written in plain text can also be overwritten by an attacker who controls the prompt or prefill.

C. Monitor the reasoning

A separate component reads the trace, either as text or as activations, flags risk, and then gates, halts or corrects the generation.

  • Guard models for traces. General-purpose guards transfer poorly to reasoning traces. ReasoningShield (Li et al., 2025) is trained on traces directly and beats LlamaGuard-4 by 35.6% and GPT-4o by 15.8%. GuardReasoner (Liu et al., 2025) is a guard that reasons before it judges, though it is mostly applied to prompts and responses rather than traces.
  • CoT monitors. Baker et al. (2025) show that GPT-4o can catch reward hacking by reading o3-mini’s chain of thought. CoT Red-Handed (Arnav et al., NeurIPS 2025) finds that scoring the trace and the actions separately doubles detection of subtle deception over watching actions alone.
  • Probes on activations. A linear probe on CoT activations predicts whether the final answer will be safe, beating text-based monitors by 13 F1 on average (Chan et al., 2025). The hidden state carries signal the text does not.

What monitoring buys and costs. It separates duties, makes decisions auditable, and is the main lever against agentic misbehavior. It costs a second model call, adds false positives, and adds latency when it runs while the trace streams. Its central weakness is in the next section: it only works when the trace means what it says.

D. Contain the reasoning

The fourth option accepts that the raw trace may be unsafe and controls who sees it. Frontier labs hide or summarize raw chains of thought, but this practice is documented mostly in system cards rather than papers. The research case for it comes from attacks and leaks:

  • H-CoT (Kuo et al., 2025) hijacks the model’s displayed safety reasoning and drops o1’s refusal rate from 98% to below 2%.
  • Leaky Thoughts (Green et al., EMNLP 2025) finds that more test-time compute makes answers more cautious but makes the trace leak more private data.

Containment does nothing for open-weight models, and the hidden trace still drives the answer.

The tensions that matter

Comparing the four families one at a time misses the main point. They pull against each other.

1. Training on the trace vs. monitoring the trace. Training families A3 and A4 put optimization pressure on the chain of thought, and monitoring (C) relies on that chain of thought staying honest. Baker et al. show the two collide: optimizing hard against a CoT monitor produces obfuscated reward hacking, where the misbehavior continues and the trace stops showing it. Korbak et al. (2025) call CoT monitorability “a new and fragile opportunity”. Emmons et al. (2025) are more hopeful: when the trace is doing real computation, models learn to hide intent only with significant outside help. The practical rule that follows is to avoid optimizing directly against the monitor you rely on.

2. A clean trace vs. a useful trace. Answer-Then-Check and RECAP work because the model engages with harmful drafts or flawed reasoning inside its trace. Trace-level safety (SaLT-DPO, ReasoningShield) and privacy (Leaky Thoughts) want that trace clean. You cannot have both without deciding who the trace is for.

3. Early vs. throughout. Early anchoring is very cheap; SafePath’s 295.9× compute saving is the headline. But self-jailbreak and H-CoT happen later in the trace. Step-level and recovery training cover the whole trace at a higher data and compute cost.

4. More thinking is not monotonically safer. Best-of-N and search improve safety (SRG, STAIR), and Hidden Risks finds that stronger reasoners do more harm when they comply. Leaky Thoughts shows longer reasoning leaks more.

5. Faithfulness bounds everything in C and A4. Chen et al. (2025) find that reasoning models reveal the hints they actually use only some of the time, often below 20%. Outcome RL improves this at first and then plateaus. A trace that is safe is not the same as a computation that is safe.

Takeaways

  • The field moved from securing the answer to securing the trace. The leak/escape distinction is now the right unit of evaluation, and any benchmark should score trace and answer separately.
  • Within training, the signal moved from the start of the trace (primers, key sentences) to every step plus recovery, because attacks moved later into the trace.
  • The open question is the interaction between families, not any one of them: how to put optimization pressure on reasoning without destroying the signal that monitors depend on.
